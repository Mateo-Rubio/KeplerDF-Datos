But what about cognitive training? Does it work? It’s one of the most frequently asked questions I get at my memory clinic—and, yes, there is some evidence that cognitive exercises can slow down or even prevent Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Here’s a look into how to use your brain.

Cognitive training refers to activities specifically designed to improve specific aspects of thinking ability by strengthening the parts of the brain used in these mental tasks. There are three main approaches:

1. Computerized cognitive training programs, like BrainHQ or Lumosity, have become increasingly popular for their convenience and scalability. These exercises focus on improving specific areas such as memory, attention, and executive function (the part of our brain that helps with decision-making and managing time).
2. Mindfulness meditation has been found to not only reduce stress but also improve cognitive functioning in older adults by enhancing the ability to process information efficiently, especially when it’s changing rapidly or coming from multiple sources at once. There are many resources available, including mobile apps and local classes, for people interested in giving mindfulness a try.
3. Non-pharmacological brain stimulation methods like electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) can also promote neuroplasticity—the capacity of the brain to create new connections between cells. Research is still ongoing about whether transcranial magnetic stimulation or direct electrical currents through specific regions could help maintain cognitive health, but these are promising areas for future exploration.
4. Deep brain stimulation involves surgically implanting electrodes into the brain to deliver small electric pulses that improve communication between different parts of the brain—a strategy shown to alleviate symptoms in Parkinson’s disease and might have potential benefits for people with AD as well. However, due to its invasiveness, this method is still largely experimental at present.
5. Psychedelic drugs like psilocybin (found in magic mushrooms) have shown some promise when it comes to brain health—but the evidence here is very preliminary and mostly based on small clinical trials involving people with severe AD or related cognitive disorders who are no longer responding to conventional treatments. While this may seem like a potential area of interest, significant risks remain for those without serious underlying conditions due to unknown effects these drugs might have at higher doses.
6. And finally, it’s important not to underestimate the power of exercise—not just your body but also your brain thrives on movement! Activities like walking or cycling can improve blood flow and oxygen delivery throughout your entire system, including vital organs such as your hippocampus—the part of our brains that’s heavily involved in learning, memory retention, and spatial navigation. Plus, getting outdoors is an excellent way to soothe stress!
7. The take-home message here? Engaging in targeted brain training exercises alongside mindfulness practice can be beneficial for maintaining cognitive health throughout our lifetimes—but more research needs to be done before any potential psychedelic therapies become viable options (if at all).
